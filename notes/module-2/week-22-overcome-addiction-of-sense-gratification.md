# Overcome Addiction of Sense Gratification

**His Holiness Indradyumna Swami** · Week 22 · Festivals and Kirtans · 52:08

## Summary

Lecturing on Srimad Bhagavatam 7.7.38 from the chapter 'What Prahlada Learned in the Womb,' Indradyumna Swami explains that Prahlada Maharaja puts two questions to his classmates, the sons of the demons: if pleasing the Lord is as easy as offering Him a leaf, a flower, fruit, or water in devotion, why do people not accept this simple process, and why are they instead so addicted to sense gratification? Since the Supersoul dwells in every heart as the nearest friend and well-wisher, contacting Him through hearing and chanting is effortless, whereas 'going to hell' through illicit sex, meat-eating, gambling, and intoxication actually demands enormous endeavor — building brothels, slaughterhouses, casinos, and breweries. He clarifies that Prahlada, being one of the twelve mahajanas and the crest-jewel of spiritual masters, is not ignorant but asks questions to provoke thought in materialists too absorbed to inquire, and he uses the occasion to define the bona fide guru as one who is 'heavy with knowledge,' realized (vijnana, not merely jnana) and necessarily a Vaishnava — since the gurus of yogis and jnanis can only award the 'silver or bronze medal' of mystic power or merging into Brahman, not the gold. Swami then paints the futility of material life through its four twenty-five-year stages that lead only to anxiety about death, noting the redeeming point that this very struggle breeds the humility that turns one toward God, as Bhaktivinoda Thakura maps out in Saranagati. Applying the medical model of addiction — stop the substance and replace it with something better — he says one must give up the four sinful activities and instead offer everything to Krishna and chant the holy name, warning sharply against compromise (the 'milk-fast' devotee who rationalizes eating sandesh, and dieters who regain all their weight). He closes by urging the touring devotees simply to sit and chant Hare Krishna and go out to dance in the streets, drinking the pure nectar of the holy name so that their own lives and the lives of others become perfect.

## Key Points

- The lecture is on Srimad Bhagavatam 7.7.38, where Prahlada Maharaja asks the sons of the demons two questions: why people do not accept the easy path of devotional service, and why they remain so addicted to sense gratification.
- Approaching the Lord is easy because the Supersoul (Paramatma) is seated in everyone's heart as the closest friend, reachable through the nine processes beginning with hearing and chanting; 'going to hell,' by contrast, requires great endeavor — illicit sex needs brothels, meat-eating needs slaughterhouses, gambling needs casinos, and intoxication needs breweries. Krishna accepts even a leaf, flower, fruit, or water offered with love (Bhagavad-gita 9.26).
- A real guru must be realized, not merely learned — the distinction between jnana and vijnana; Prabhupada glossed 'guru' as 'heavy with knowledge.' Gurukula children can memorize many verses yet give a blank face when asked the meaning. Chanakya Pandit's Niti-shastra verse compares a person of high birth, youth, and beauty but no knowledge to the beautiful but fragrance-less kimshuka flower, and the guru is likened to the sun, inseparable from heat and light.
- The true guru must be a Vaishnava: gurus of the yogis and jnanis can take a disciple only as far as mystic powers or merging into Brahman — the 'silver or bronze medal,' not the gold (Wimbledon Federer-vs-Nadal analogy, where second place gets half the money and fame). Prahlada, one of the twelve mahajanas, is the crest-jewel of gurus.
- Great teachers deliberately take the role of the student to show materialists, who are too absorbed to inquire, how to ask the right questions (Bhagavad-gita 4.34, 'tad viddhi pranipatena'). Examples of proper inquiry: the sages of Naimisharanya to Suta Goswami, Pariksit to Sukadeva, Rupa and Sanatana to Lord Chaitanya (Sanatana's 'ke ami — who am I?'), and even Chaitanya inquiring from Haridasa Thakura and Ramananda Raya.
- Material life is futile: 25 years studying, 25 working to maintain family, 25 warding off disease and old age, and any remaining time spent in anxiety about death — money does not grow on trees and most of us are not Bill Gates. Its one good side is that repeated defeat breeds humility (dainya), the first step of surrender, which Bhaktivinoda Thakura develops through the seven songs of Saranagati (from cradle to grave, then atma-nivedana, accepting Krishna as protector, and rejecting the unfavorable); of the four kinds of pious men who turn to Krishna (Bhagavad-gita 7.16), the distressed (arta) are the main group.
- Addiction is dependence one is powerless to stop; doctors treat it by stopping the substance and replacing it with something better (stop smoking, chew gum). Likewise one stops sense gratification and offers everything to Krishna (Bhagavad-gita 9.27, 'yat karoshi yad ashnasi') and chants constantly (Siksastakam, 'kirtaniya sada hari'). No compromise — the 'milk fast' (payovrata) devotee who also ate sandesh, burfi, and ice cream 'because they are milk products,' and the 70% of dieters who regain their weight, illustrate the danger of sliding back; drink the pure nectar of the holy name (Bhaktivinoda Thakura's Gitavali).

## Scriptural References

- Srimad Bhagavatam 7.7.38 — the verse under discussion, in which Prahlada Maharaja, addressing the sons of the demons, asks why people do not engage in the easy devotional service of the ever-present Supersoul and why they are instead 'addicted to unnecessarily producing artificial paraphernalia for sense gratification.'
- Bhagavad-gita 9.26 ('patram pushpam phalam toyam yo me bhaktya prayacchati...') — cited that the Lord accepts even a leaf, a flower, fruit, or water offered with love and devotion, showing how easy it is to please Him.
- Bhagavad-gita 9.27 ('yat karoshi yad ashnasi yaj juhoshi dadasi yat / yat tapasyasi kaunteya tat kurushva mad-arpanam') — quoted as the cure for addiction: whatever one does, eats, offers, gives away, or performs as austerity should be done for Krishna.
- Bhagavad-gita 4.34 ('tad viddhi pranipatena pariprashnena sevaya') — cited on the disciple's relationship with the guru: approach him by submission, relevant inquiry, and service (question and answer).
- Bhagavad-gita 7.16 — the four kinds of pious men who render devotional service: the distressed (arta), the desirer of wealth (artharthi), the inquisitive (jijnasu), and the seeker of knowledge (jnani); Prabhupada notes the distressed are the main group who surrender.
- Srimad Bhagavatam 7.5.23 — the nine processes of devotional service ('sravanam kirtanam vishnoh smaranam pada-sevanam / archanam vandanam dasyam sakhyam atma-nivedanam'), quoted as the easy means of coming in touch with the Lord.
- Srimad Bhagavatam 2.1.2 — Sukadeva Goswami's description of materially engrossed persons who, being blind to the knowledge of the ultimate truth, have many subject matters for hearing in human society; cited as a glimpse into the mind of the materialist.
- Srimad Bhagavatam 1.19 / 2.1 — Maharaja Pariksit's question to Sukadeva Goswami on the duty of one about to die, which Sukadeva praised as a glorious question 'approved by all transcendentalists.'
- Brahma-samhita 5.1 ('ishvarah paramah krishnah sac-cid-ananda-vigrahah / anadir adir govindah sarva-karana-karanam') — cited as the siddhanta (conclusion) that a real guru knows and imparts.
- Chanakya Pandit, Niti-shastra ('rupa-yauvana-sampanna vishala-kula-sambhava / vidya-hina na shobhante nirgandha iva kimshukah') — a person of beauty, youth, and high birth without knowledge is unattractive, like the beautiful but fragrance-less kimshuka (palasha) flower.
- Mundaka Upanishad (referenced as 'the Upanishads') — to learn transcendental subject matter one must approach a spiritual master carrying fuel for sacrifice; the guru's symptom is 'shrotriyam' (expert in the Vedic conclusion) and being always engaged in devotional service.
- Siksastakam verse 3 ('kirtaniyah sada harih') — quoted for chanting the holy name constantly, in contrast to sense gratification.
- Narottama Dasa Thakura, Sri Guru-vandana ('cakhu-dana dila yei, janme janme prabhu sei') — sung every morning: the guru gives the gift of transcendental vision and, by his mercy, divine knowledge is revealed in the heart.
- Bhaktivinoda Thakura, Saranagati — the collection whose songs move from the frustration of life 'from the cradle to the grave' through dainya (humility, the first quality of surrender), atma-nivedana (self-dedication), accepting Krishna as protector/maintainer ('avashya rakshibe krishna'), and rejecting activities unfavorable to devotional service.
- Bhaktivinoda Thakura, Gitavali — cited for the joyful process of chanting: the line that life becomes successful when one is attached to the holy name (without affection for Krishna life is useless, and at the end such a chanter obtains the vision of Radha-Shyama in Vrindavan), and 'drink the pure nectar of the holy name... except for the holy name there is nothing within all the fourteen worlds.'
- Srila Prabhupada's poem/prayer — 'Struggle for existence, the human race, the only hope is divine grace,' cited on how the hardships of material life turn one, in humility, toward God.
- Srimad Bhagavatam (referenced) — Prahlada Maharaja named as one of the twelve mahajanas, establishing his authority as a spiritual master rather than a confused inquirer.

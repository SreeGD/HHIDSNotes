# Lecture on Narottam Das Thakur’s Appearance Day

**His Holiness Indradyumna Swami** · Week 57 · Festivals and Kirtans · 1:12:26

## Summary

Delivered on the appearance day of Narottama Dasa Thakura, this lecture presents him as one of the three great acharyas (with Shyamananda and Srinivasa Acharya) who carried on Mahaprabhu's sankirtana movement after the Panchatattva departed. Though an eternally liberated soul (nitya-siddha) who wrote only two books, Narottama exemplified the perfect disciple and sadhaka, and Indradyumna Swami stresses that acharyas, and even the Lord in His incarnations, descend to set an example, including how to accept and serve a spiritual master. He recounts how Narottama, a Bengali prince of Kheturi, was directed in a dream by Lord Nityananda to bathe in the river where Chaitanya Mahaprabhu had deposited His prema, and then instructed by Mahaprabhu to take shelter of Lokanatha Goswami in Vrindavan, renouncing his throne and betrothed bride with the words that, having given his heart to Radha and Krishna, he could give it to no one else. Central stories illustrate humility and menial service: Lokanatha Goswami at first refused to initiate him ('I am not even a devotee'), and Narottama secretly cleaned his guru's latrine until discovered, thereby capturing his guru's heart. The Swami weaves in the Bhagavad-gita method of approaching a guru (pranipata, pariprasna, seva) and Rupa Goswami's progression from shraddha through sadhu-sanga, bhajana-kriya, and anartha-nivritti, arguing that real advancement requires sacrifice suited to one's station: travel and preaching for the renounced, and charity for householders (his refrain 'cut your darlings'). He also emphasizes that diksha and shiksha gurus are equal, citing how Narottama, Shyamananda, and Srinivasa studied together under Jiva Goswami despite different diksha gurus as a model of unity in diversity for ISKCON. He closes with the gopis as the supreme model of surrender, who abandoned reputation, family, and social standing at the sound of Krishna's flute, showing that service to guru and Krishna must be one's very life and soul rather than a token offering.

## Key Points

- Narottama Dasa Thakura, though a nitya-siddha who produced only two books (including Prema-bhakti-chandrika), was a perfect acharya who, with Shyamananda and Srinivasa Acharya, carried forward Mahaprabhu's sankirtana movement after the Panchatattva departed.
- Acharyas, and even the Lord's incarnations, descend to set an example, including how to accept and serve a guru (Krishna accepted Sandipani Muni; Chaitanya accepted Ishvara Puri as diksha guru and Keshava Bharati as sannyasa guru).
- Narottama's guru-selection: Lord Nityananda in a dream sent him to bathe in the river where Mahaprabhu had deposited His prema, after which Mahaprabhu directed him to Lokanatha Goswami; he renounced his royal inheritance and arranged marriage, telling his father's soldiers he had given his heart only to Radha and Krishna.
- Menial service and humility endear a disciple to the guru: Lokanatha Goswami refused to initiate him out of humility, and Narottama secretly cleaned his guru's latrine until caught, illustrating that the quality of seva outweighs its quantity.
- Diksha and shiksha gurus are equal; Narottama, Shyamananda, and Srinivasa studied together under Jiva Goswami despite having different diksha gurus, a model of cooperation and 'unity in diversity' for ISKCON.
- The path of surrender follows the Bhagavad-gita (pranipata, pariprasna, seva) and Rupa Goswami's stages (shraddha, sadhu-sanga, bhajana-kriya, anartha-nivritti), and demands sacrifice suited to one's ashrama: preaching and travel for the renounced, giving hard-earned money for grihasthas.
- The gopis exemplify total surrender, giving up reputation (harder to renounce than sex or wealth), family, and safety at the sound of Krishna's flute, proving that devotional service must be one's life and soul, not token service.

## Scriptural References

- Bhagavad-gita 4.34 ('tad viddhi pranipatena pariprasnena sevaya... upadekshyanti te jnanam jnaninas tattva-darshinah') - the method of approaching a guru through submission, inquiry, and service, and 'tattva-darshinah' as those who have seen the truth; the lecture's recurring theme of 'pariprasna'.
- Bhagavad-gita 9.26 ('patram pushpam phalam toyam') - the Lord accepts a leaf, flower, fruit, or water offered with devotion; cited for quality of service over quantity.
- Bhagavad-gita 8.15 ('duhkhalayam ashashvatam') - the material world as a temporary abode of suffering; cited for seeing the world as it truly is.
- Bhagavad-gita 16.21 - the three gates of hell (lust, anger, greed); paraphrased as 'the three gates leading to hell' in the discussion of anarthas.
- Bhakti-rasamrita-sindhu 1.4.15-16 (Rupa Goswami) ('adau shraddha tatah sadhu-sango 'tha bhajana-kriya, tato 'nartha-nivrittih syat') - the sequential stages of devotional development; cited via 'sadhu-sanga... bhajana-kriya... anartha-nivrittih syat'.
- Brahma-samhita 5.1 ('ishvarah paramah krishnah sac-cid-ananda-vigrahah, anadir adir govindah') - definition of Krishna as the Supreme; quoted when describing the guru-disciple inquiry into who is the Supreme Soul.
- Brahma-samhita 5.38 ('premanjana-cchurita-bhakti-vilochanena') - the devotee sees Krishna through eyes anointed with the salve of love (transcribed as 'eyes covered by the sun').
- Chaitanya-charitamrita (Krishnadasa Kaviraja Goswami) - the maxim paraphrased as 'elegance is truth spoken concisely' (exact verse not identified), cited to explain Narottama's brief yet profound writings, likened to the coded brevity of Vedanta-sutra.

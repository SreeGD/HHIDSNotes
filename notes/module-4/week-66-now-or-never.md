# Now or Never

**His Holiness Indradyumna Swami** · Week 66 ·  · 2:14:00

**Themes:** soul versus body · reincarnation and karma · vegetarianism and ahimsa · chanting the holy name · the bona fide guru · liberation (mukti)

## Summary

Speaking to a university audience at an ISKCON festival, Indradyumna Swami introduces the philosophy of the Bhagavad Gita, presenting it not as a new religion but as one of humanity's oldest spiritual traditions and an 'unabridged spiritual dictionary' that can answer any question about spiritual life. His core theme is that the human form, uniquely endowed with intelligence (buddhi), is the rare chance to inquire into the self ('athato brahma jijnasa') and to act on it now. He argues that we are not the material body (yantra, a machine) but the eternal soul (atma) that drives it, contrasting a whole life spent 'polishing the car' with neglecting the driver inside, and explains transmigration of the soul, karma, and the spiritual equality of all living beings. This equality grounds his case for vegetarianism, which he illustrates with the story of how, as a fourteen-year-old football and swim-team captain, a single visit to a slaughterhouse arranged by his vegetarian father made him give up meat on the spot. He describes the material world as a temporary place of duality and suffering, a 'prison' where even Bill Gates cannot escape birth, disease, old age and death, and names mukti (liberation), the return to a loving relationship with a personal God, as life's real goal. The practical process he recommends is chanting the Hare Krishna maha-mantra, which awakens the sleeping soul like an alarm clock, supported by a disciplined daily life (rising in the brahma-muhurta, sixteen rounds of japa on 108 beads, Bhagavad Gita study) and four regulative principles: no meat, no intoxication, no illicit sex, and no gambling. In the extended Q&A he explains that vegetarianism is completed by offering food to Krishna as prasadam (making it karma-free), defends the need for a bona fide guru as God's empowered representative (a 'telescope' that brings the distant moon near), and clarifies that self-realization is not merging into an impersonal void but reawakening one of several eternal loving relationships (rasa) with God, as servant, friend, parent, or lover.

## Introduction — An Ancient Tradition, Not Something New

- Welcome to this evening's program at the university. The speaker is himself a guest, called to share a little of the philosophy and lifestyle of the culture being followed, which comes from India.
- When people first see members of the society — technically the **International Society for Krishna Consciousness (ISKCON)**, more popularly the **Hare Krishna Movement** — on the street in robes, chanting, dancing, and singing, or distributing books, or at a program like this, they think it must be some brand-new spiritual tradition. This couldn't be further from the truth.
- It is one of the oldest spiritual traditions known to man, practiced in India for **over 5,000 years** — predating the Egyptian civilization (the pyramids and pharaohs) by about 2,000 years.
- It is now accepted in Western countries as an authentic spiritual tradition, most notably because it is based on a very ancient text — Sanskrit **śāstra**, Western "scripture." Though it only came to the West about 35 years ago (when the spiritual teacher came from India), professors at great universities now accept it as bona fide.

## The Bhagavad-gītā

- One of India's oldest and most well-known scriptures, followed by most people in India: the **Bhagavad-gītā.** *Gītā* means "song," *Bhagavad* refers to the Supreme Lord or God — literally, "the Song of God." It was spoken by **Lord Kṛṣṇa** when He was present on this planet 5,000 years ago. About 700 verses of concise spiritual knowledge.
- It has been compared to an **unabridged spiritual dictionary.** As dictionaries are abridged (few words) or unabridged (lots of words): in a comparative study with the world's great scriptures (the Bible, the Koran, the Torah, the Lotus Sūtras of Buddha), the Gītā is unique in that it can answer any question you have about spiritual life. We should all have questions of that nature — that is what distinguishes us from all other species.

### Life Throughout the Universe
- The Gītā is very specific: within this material world there are exactly **8,400,000 species of life.** Modern science has found about 3,200,000. But the Gītā addresses the whole universe, solar system, and cosmic manifestation — there is life throughout the universe, not only on this planet.
- **Story — the mullah in Tajikistan:** The speaker travels and spends time in Islamic countries, undercover (not dressed like this, "because I get in trouble"), sharing the ancient teachings. His close friend in Tajikistan is a mullah — a renounced monk in their tradition. Rather than seeing differences, they see what they have in common. Discussing life in the universe, the mullah said: "Maharaj, if there's not life all over the universe, all over the cosmos, that means that Allah wasted a lot of space." A good point — "I'll use that in one of my lectures."
- It may not be life as we know it, but that doesn't mean life can't exist elsewhere. There are living entities in the ocean, though you can't live in water longer than about five minutes; certain viruses can even survive fire (they can only be terminated by chemicals). Just because you can't live on other planets doesn't mean, by God's arrangement, there aren't other living entities there. Otherwise, why all the planets and solar systems — just for big space? No.

### One Source of All Life
- Out of all these millions of living entities — whom we should all respect, because life comes ultimately from one source, God, the supreme creator — different traditions have different names, like the sun: one sun, but France says *La Soleil*, Spain *El Sol*, in Hindi *Sūrya, Sūrya Bhagavān.* Different names, one sun. So too God is one person with many names, the source of life.
- **BG 10.8** (*ahaṁ sarvasya prabhavo mattaḥ sarvaṁ pravartate*): Kṛṣṇa is the source of all that is material and spiritual. Because all life comes from one source, we are all brothers and sisters. (In the '60s in San Francisco "we were trying to understand how we're all brothers and sisters" — because we have a common Father.) A saintly person respects all living entities.

## The Purpose of Human Intelligence (Buddhi)

- Special emphasis is given to human life because we have one thing other species don't: **buddhi.** ("You've heard of *Buddha*" — meaning both the enlightened one and the most intelligent one.) Buddhi means intelligence, far superior to any other species — evident because there are no cats and dogs sitting in the seats or teaching in this university.
- The ultimate purpose of that intelligence is **athāto brahma jijñāsā** — to go within and discover your spiritual nature. That takes a lot of intelligence, because the **ātmā** (soul) is so subtle it cannot be perceived by any instruments — microscopes, x-rays. There is a relative purpose (to make life easier), but the ultimate purpose is to discover the self within. That is real progress, because the body is here today, gone tomorrow.
- **BG 2.20** (paraphrased): "For the soul there is never birth nor death; nor, having once been, does he ever cease to be. He is unborn, undying, primeval; he is not slain when the body is slain."
- The subject matter of the book is: **Who are you?** It is important to know who you are before you start your life. Bob Marley addressed it in his songs: "We know who we are, we know where we're going. Exodus. Out of here." It is the same universal truth in any tradition — Rasta, Christian, Hindu, Jew, Muslim: **this is not our real home.**

## You Are Not the Body

- The first step in the science of self-realization is to understand that force within — the spark of life, the soul that animates the body. In the second chapter Kṛṣṇa gives the revolutionary concept that **we are not these material bodies.**
- We are in the bodily concept: in the morning at the mirror, the boys flexing ("I'm looking really good"), the girls ("very nice"). This is a misconception. In Sanskrit the body is **yantra** — a machine, a very sophisticated machine made of material energy; the driver of the machine is the **ātmā**, the spark of life, the soul within. This is basic knowledge in India — even the street sweeper knows he is not the body — but in the West we are very much in the bodily concept.
- You are not the body, if only because **matter doesn't have life.** The material things in this room are sophisticated but not conscious, not living; matter is inherently dead, and it takes a living person to move it. The body is made of the same matter, chemicals, atoms, electrons, neutrons, quarks as anything else in the room — dead. Is the skin living? The muscles? The bones? (Only in scary movies does the skeleton walk.) What gives it the semblance of life is the driver within — like a car, a sophisticated machine that is useless without a conscious driver.
- **Intuitively, we know we are not the body:** we say "this is *my* hand, *my* arm, *my* head, *my* body — it belongs to me." Who are you? "I'm the body." No — you said it's *your* body; you didn't say "I-hand, I-arm, I-head." (You wouldn't get into this university talking like that.)
- We are into the body — okay, like you're into your car — but there is a difference between polishing the car and feeding the guy inside. We spend our whole life polishing the body but neglect the driver. Anyone could speak half an hour about the car (how tall you are, your eye color, your weight), but what about the driver? Can you speak even five minutes about who you really are, or your real interest as an ātmā? We're so advanced in science and technology, yet we can't.

### The Soul's Dimension and Consciousness
- The Gītā explains that the spark of life, the person in the body, is **one ten-thousandth the tip of a hair in dimension** — very small. How does he manipulate the body? Look at the sun: tiny compared to the universe, but its heat and rays reach all the way to Pluto and beyond — very powerful.
- The soul is situated within the region of the heart and is present throughout the body **by consciousness.** As heat and light represent the sun, so consciousness is the symptom of the soul, spread throughout the body. When we talk about ourselves, we point to the chest ("I want to tell you"), not the head, because intuitively we know these things.
- We just don't have enough teachers speaking this knowledge — we should have teachers circulating throughout society giving non-sectarian spiritual enlightenment. Nobody can monopolize this knowledge; it is deeper spiritual truth.

## The Soul Enters the Body — Reincarnation

- The particle of soul comes into the body at the moment of conception, when the sperm and ovum meet — it's like creating life. In biology class we hear that under favorable conditions certain chemicals come together and produce life. But that is not very scientific: **science means hypothesis, experiment, and result.** Nothing is reliable truth until the experiment is carried out and gives the result. Otherwise it is just theory, and no intelligent man accepts theory as knowledge.
- The hypothesis is that life comes from chemicals. So carry out the experiment — you don't even have to create something as sophisticated as a human being; just create an amoeba, a virus, a fly, or, if you're really smart, a chicken. But no one has ever done it. "We're going to do it" is like a **post-dated check** — writing a check for a million dollars dated July 21st, 2900. Who would accept it? Life has no beginning and no end; it is eternal.
- By higher arrangement, by providence, the soul is attracted to the womb. It is a great science: in India there is **Garbhādhāna-saṁskāra**, where before sex the husband and wife prepare their minds very spiritually so as to attract an elevated soul to the womb — a lost art (this is not a sex-education class).
- **Analogy — the apartment:** The ovum and sperm create a "house," and somebody moves in. (Driving here, the speaker saw new apartments going up because 3,000 more students are being accepted; the block is built and students move in.) Because of the presence of the ātmā, the body grows — dead things don't grow, only living things. The soul is there in its entirety, but the house is still being constructed. This is why in India there is no abortion — life is accepted from the very beginning. At **seven months** the soul becomes fully conscious; at **nine months** there is birth, then infancy, childhood, young adulthood, middle age. As long as the driver remains in the car, it keeps growing; take the soul out, and life is finished — that's death.

### What Is Death?
- We hope to live to a ripe old age of 70, 80, 90 (by the grace of science — "we don't want to slam science too much"). Death simply means the soul exits. As the symptom that the sun has expired is no more heat and light, the symptom that the soul has left is no more consciousness. Even you know this: you think the person is the body, but you say, "They've gone." What's gone? See how ignorant we are — we have only half knowledge, and a little knowledge is sometimes more dangerous than none.
- The intelligent next question: **where does the soul go?** Whether American, Canadian, German, French, Hindu, Christian, Jew — black, white, yellow — this is an essential question for everybody. "Hey, I'm young, don't bring me down, I can think about that later." But don't young people also die? The statistics are sober; you can't be like the ostrich that digs a hole and sticks its head in to forget the hunter. You've got to pay your taxes, and you have to die.
- You *are* concerned about your future — otherwise why are you at school, giving the best years of your life (when you're young you're physically healthy and the mind and intelligence are sharp)? You graduate at 25 or 30, get a career and family, put money aside, give it to the government and Social Security, and hope the economy is good enough to take care of you after 65. But we're like the horse with blinders — we don't think beyond 50 or 60 years. Not very smart. Maybe the scientists are right that life is finished at death — few people can really accept that — but either way it's a profound subject you should look into.
- **Analogy — the bulldozer:** As we get involved in electing a president because the future of the country matters, so there are life-changing decisions to make — and here's one: where am I going? They build an apartment building, and after a few years the city council decides to put a supermarket there, giving six months' notice to bulldoze. What do you do? You get the classified ads, find an apartment you can afford, make a down payment, and a couple of days before the bulldozer you're out and into your new place. You don't just sit there. You have to make some investment — it's not nice, but neither is paying taxes, and you have to.

## Science and Religion — A Happy Synthesis

- This book is a synthesis of science and religion — not a competitive discussion, a happy synthesis. As the spiritual master said: **"Religion without philosophy is just sentiment, and philosophy without religion is mere mental speculation."** India is the synthesis of science and religion.
- **BG 2.13** (what happens when we die): "As the embodied soul passes from the body of a baby to a boy, to a youth, to an old man, the soul passes into another body at death." Back in the womb — like a student who doesn't study and doesn't pass his final exams has to come back next year.
- In the universal law, if you don't get the point — if you don't graduate from the material world, meaning become enlightened, God conscious, a lover of God, and go back to the real (spiritual) world — then you come back, again and again.
- **Story — friends still in school:** When the speaker was young he went to university and (having a sentiment that the war wasn't right) joined the military; coming back after a few years, his friends were still in school. "What are you guys doing?" "It's safe here, we've got an apartment." They wanted to keep coming back. Shakespeare called it the school of life: if you don't graduate, you're born again and again — the laws of nature, not philosophy or religious dogma, just the facts of life.
- **Proof that we change bodies:** A boy at another school asked for proof. "You're changing bodies even in this lifetime." Medical science says that every seven years all the cells in the body change, even the brain — so every seven years you have a completely new body, but are you a different person? No, the same person. You remember when you were five (the girls playing with dolls, the boys with trucks) — where is your five-year-old body? And when you return to your university (*alma mater*) as an old alumnus, gray-haired, wrinkled, with a cane, they honor you as the same student, though you have a completely different body. Something is changing; something is remaining the same. When the car is old and useless you give it up; when the body is old and useless the soul abandons it.

### The Soul Can Take Lower Births
- According to his **karma** (his actions and their subsequent reactions), one is placed in the womb of a new mother — and not necessarily the human species. This is a radical idea for Westerners. In India we accept that all living creatures have a soul equal to yours.
- **BG 5.18** (paraphrased): "A truly learned person sees with equal vision a learned and gentle brāhmaṇa, an elephant, a cow, a dog, and a dog-eater." Different dresses, different bodies, but the soul is equal.
- If you say it can't be, how do you explain the consciousness within the creature? You eat, the dog eats; you sleep, the dog sleeps; you mate, the dog mates; you fight, the dog fights; you feel fear, so does the dog; you feel love and affection, so does the dog; you use intelligence, and the dog is intelligent (take the stick and he moves out of the way). The similarities are across the board — only our intelligence is more advanced. Babies aren't very intelligent either, but that doesn't mean they're not spirit souls.
- You don't change the batteries in your dog every week or put petrol in him; he's not like your washing machine. When you come home from vacation, your washing machine doesn't jump out and lick you, and your toaster doesn't start popping toast — but your pets do. And when you go away, you're concerned about your pets, because they're living.

## Vegetarianism and Equality

- In India, people are vegetarian because if somebody pinches you it hurts, and if you pinch an animal it also squeals or cries or barks. The ethic (the speaker was brought up Roman Catholic) is to do unto others as we would have them do unto us. Why just men and women? Why apartheid?
- **South Africa / apartheid:** The speaker spent many years in South Africa as a member of this movement, fighting apartheid, because it was wrong that the black man, the Indian people, and the colored people had fewer privileges than the white man — they were really abused. He can't see how anyone could support the system as it was before Mandela ("a very good friend of mine — we took lunch together many times and discussed these things"). The same principle: **it is apartheid to think you are superior to any of God's creatures.** You're not better than them, you're more fortunate — and therefore we shouldn't kill them. There's no need to eat meat.
- **Story — the slaughterhouse:** As a boy of 14, captain of his football team (quarterback) and captain of the swim team, the speaker insisted "I've got to be strong." His father, a vegetarian, kept telling him to stop; so his father took him to the slaughterhouse one Sunday morning — and he stopped right then and there.
- **The hamburger myth:** He once saw a McDonald's commercial where little kids were picking hamburgers off trees. Hamburgers don't grow on trees — they come from slaughterhouses. The myth that you need meat to be strong isn't true: the elephant, the strongest animal, is vegetarian; the bull is vegetarian (if a bull walked in, even the mixed-martial-arts experts would run out). The speaker has been vegetarian since 14 (now 60), healthy, and his students can hardly keep up with him. **George Bernard Shaw:** "You are what you eat." And "if you live by the gun, you die by the gun" — that's karma.
- Animals have life; they're living, covered much more than we are, without the intelligence to understand anything beyond satisfying their senses — but so do crippled people and "retarded" people, and we take care of them. We should take care of all creatures.
- **The real platform for peace:** Peace means seeing not the differences (skin color, language, country of birth) but what we have in common. Throw rocks into different parts of a pond and the waves clash; if all the rocks fall at the same point, there's no clashing. For peace in the world we need one united philosophy: we are all spirit souls, all equal — men, women, blacks, "yellow, greens," animals — no partial apartheid. Clear the slate, love one another, and treat one another as we would like to be treated.

## Hell, Higher and Lower Births, and the Prison of Duality

- Because of impious activities the soul can go temporarily into lower species — that is hell. Hell may also be a place of burning in a lake of fire (he won't comment), but really, **hell is where you are forgetful of God.** You can have it well in this world, but if you're an atheist you're living in hell — because real happiness comes not from material objects but from within, when you rediscover your lost relationship with the Supreme Personality of Godhead.
- The soul can take birth in lower species, again as a human, or on higher realms — **Svargaloka**, the heavenly planets (not "heaven," but higher planets). But wherever you take birth, it's a bad deal. Like a lifer in the penitentiary who says, "I've got TV, an iPod, my girlfriend visits once a month, I've got it good" — you're still in prison, buddy. A first-class prisoner, someone else second- or third-class — whatever you are, you're still in the prison house of material existence. You can't be perfectly happy here because there is **birth, disease, old age, and death,** and science will never stop it — it can adjust and ease it, but not stop it.
- **The intrinsic nature of the soul:** As the intrinsic nature of salt is salty, of sugar sweet, of water wet — what is the intrinsic nature of that spark of life in the chest, the real you? You discover it in this book: the nature of the soul is **to always be seeking happiness.** We all want to be happy (with different conceptions of happiness), and that is a motivating factor. But the Gītā points out you can't find perfect happiness here, because this is a **world of duality:** an hour ago it was day, now it's night; heat and cold, up and down, heads and tails, happiness and distress. We're not naïve — there's plenty of happiness, but also plenty of distress.
- **Even in America:** The speaker has traveled to every country in the world without exception; America is his homeland and a great place (religious freedom, political speech — elsewhere he can't say a peep about the government or that he practices a tradition from India). But the same rule of duality applies. Do you think people in fancy homes and big cars aren't in anxiety? Sometimes they're the most anxious — afraid the government will tax them to death, that thieves will come; they also get cancer and AIDS, and their teenage daughters also get pregnant. In this material world there is no place for a lady or a gentleman.
- But we're here, so we have to make the best use of a bad bargain. There's a way to live — **simple living and high thinking** — so you're not so disturbed or stressed. That's not the ultimate goal, though; you live intelligently. You're not the body, but you should take care of it — as you're not the car but you maintain it because it takes you to your destination.
- The goal is not education, career, family, retirement, and a condo in Florida — those are relative goals. The higher goal is **mukti, liberation** — not a concept invented by old sages in the forests of India; the goal of life is the same now as 5,000 years ago and 5,000 years from now: to go back to the spiritual world from whence we came. We're like fish out of water. Even Bill Gates will get old, diseased, and die — it's like a dream. So we must find some process whereby, in the midst of daily life, we put a little time aside for investment in our emancipation from material existence.
- Because there are different strokes for different folks, there are different religions for different types of people — and no one should slam anybody else's religion. The speaker was called here to share India's tradition; even as a Westerner he adopted it as a young man of 19, because he was always looking for spiritual knowledge and found the Gītā so deep, explicit, definite, and logical — everything explained (the soul, the Supreme Person, the creation, the spiritual world), no stone left unturned. Plus there is a whole lifestyle conducive to realizing this knowledge: as a businessman associates with like-minded people, a spiritual person lives in a society of like-minded people. All over the world the movement has temples, ashrams, schools, farms, and restaurants — a whole culture to take advantage of part-time or full-time.
- **"This is Indian, I'm American — it's foreign."** Where did Christianity come from? New York? It came from the East, as most great traditions do. It's a small world; America is a melting pot of cultures — why not India in a spiritual way? Was pizza invented in New Orleans? Pizza is from Rome, Italy — but imagine if President Bush banned pizza; they'd impeach him. Smallpox vaccination came from France. Here's something from a so-called foreign country that will make you a better person — physically healthy, intellectually sharp, mentally clear, and spiritually enlightened — and you don't even have to change your religion. ("I don't feel that I ever gave up my Roman Catholic roots; I just evolved in that same concept of becoming more and more God conscious.")

There are different mantras, but this is the great mantra for deliverance from material existence.

It's on sale in the back (not to be sold from the mic). "If we didn't include the philosophy of ancient India in this program, we wouldn't be doing the culture any real justice." Thank you for tolerating me and for coming. Hare Kṛṣṇa.

## The Abhiṣeka — Deity Worship Is Not Idol Worship

- There will now be an **Abhiṣeka**, one of the rituals in India's temples (as the West has church rituals) by which priests and the faithful focus on God and develop a relationship with Him.
- On the altar in India are **Deities** of the Supreme Lord. Westerners see the Deities (mūrti forms) and think it is idol worship — but it is not. Idol worship means to concoct some idea of what God might be like and worship that form. But in the Vedic scriptures there is a very detailed description of what God actually looks like. This is revolutionary in the West, where we talk about God but don't really know what He's like; in India there is a detailed description of the spiritual world, the souls who live there, and ultimately of the Supreme Personality of Godhead Himself.
- There is an injunction in the ancient scriptures that you can carve that bona fide form of God (not speculation, not some golden calf, not some idea) and worship it as the Lord Himself, because through a certain **installation process** the Lord agrees to appear in that Deity form to accept our worship. As an expert electrician can make a material object hot or cold by electricity, God can turn spirit into matter or matter into spirit — He is all-powerful.
- For thousands of years Deity worship has been integral to India's religious process: going to the temple, praying to the Deity, bathing and dressing the Deity, making food offerings, offering flowers, incense, and lamps — and thereby the Lord accepts our worship through the medium of the Deity. It is not idol worship, not like worshiping a downtown statue of George Washington; there is a very deep philosophy behind it, and those who worship the Deity make genuine spiritual advancement.
- (While the Abhiṣeka takes place there will be more kīrtan to increase the auspiciousness of the atmosphere, then an ārati ceremony, then prasādam is served.)

## Questions and Answers — First, the Daily Lifestyle

- (Inviting questions: "One time our guru said, no questions means everyone accepts.") In the absence of a first question, the speaker describes the daily lifestyle.
- The movement follows the ancient spiritual tradition of India — like the yogīs, ṛṣis, and sādhus — to the letter. We start the day very early. In India's villages (not so much in Mumbai, "like a little New York") people rise about **3:30 or 4:00 — Brahma-muhūrta**, an hour and a half before sunrise. The day is understood by different junctures, and that time is the most auspicious for spiritual activities: everything is quiet, the common man is asleep, no traffic, no marketplace open — perfect for meditation.
- We get up and take a shower ("cleanliness is next to godliness — or it *is* godliness"). Celibate monks (not married, without wife, children, or job, maintained by the congregation) wear **saffron**; married people wear **white.** Neither has an advantage over the other; it's a choice of how you'd like to live. Saffron represents fire, which burns up material desires — to leave this world you have to give up material desires (for name, fame, material objects, enjoyment) in order to awaken deeper spiritual desires for service to God.
- We go into the temple room (in the house or down the street) and begin the day singing hymns glorifying the guru.

### Approaching a Spiritual Master — BG 4.34
- **BG 4.34** (*tad viddhi praṇipātena paripraśnena sevayā, upadekṣyanti te jñānaṁ jñāninas tattva-darśinaḥ*): Kṛṣṇa addresses the whole society, not just India. *Darśana* means "to see," *tattva* means "truth." Learn spiritual truths by approaching a spiritual master, inquiring from him submissively, and rendering service to him; self-realized souls can give you the truth because they have seen the truth.
- It's like any subject — business, chemistry, music: it's much easier if you approach a teacher; practicing on your own, you won't get far. Why should spiritual knowledge be any different? In every tradition there are learned people who share their knowledge; in India they are **brāhmaṇas**, the priestly class, meant to elevate people from the mode of ignorance to passion up to the mode of **goodness**, which is more favorable for understanding spiritual subject matter.
- At some point we accept a guru. A guru isn't just a guy with long hair, flowing robes, and a twinkle in his eye — that's not the qualification. A real guru represents the Absolute Truth both in his speaking and his deeds; he isn't making something up like a charismatic person with good ideas. The perfect ideas are already there, given by God in the scriptures — you can't do better than that. So a real spiritual master repeats, for example, the Bhagavad-gītā as it is, and demonstrates that philosophy in his life. By practicing a spiritual way of life (not a hobby or club) he actually becomes self-realized, getting deep realizations; one who has achieved some perfection can become a teacher and help others.
- We are grateful and indebted to the teacher, because he is teaching us not just knowledge to get along in this world, but how to get out of this world and into the spiritual world.
- **The "escapist" objection:** A boy said, "You guys are just escapists, escaping society and responsibility." First, we do live in society — most of our members work, with jobs, homes, and families; only a few like me travel and teach, and we're not irresponsible. "Yes, we are trying to escape." "See?" "No — if you're in a house and it starts to burn down and I run outside, and you call me an escapist, is there any logic to that? You're crazy if you don't try to escape." The material world is always in flux, in a state of deterioration — all the great empires, kingdoms, people, and ideas have vanished in time. It's a scary place, so we should try to escape; it's more responsible to go back to our original home than to try to make sense of this madness. **BG 8.15** — *duḥkhālayam aśāśvatam*, a place that is temporary and full of suffering. We owe that understanding to our spiritual teacher.
- Someone who helps you understand your real self, and the Supreme Self, God — who is not a light or an energy but actually a **person**, a spiritual person with a form, not an old man with gray hair, hunched over. He is described in Sanskrit as **nava-yauvanam** — eternally youthful. In the ancient paintings of India, God/Kṛṣṇa is very young, like a youth of 16 or 17, and He remains like that forever, very beautiful, full of all opulences.

### The Chanting of 16 Rounds
- We begin the day singing hymns to the spiritual master, then sit down with meditation beads. There are **108 beads.** Where the mantra is sung loudly in kīrtan, here we sit in a yoga posture (āsana) so the mind is peaceful and controlled, and softly turn each bead chanting *Hare Kṛṣṇa Hare Kṛṣṇa, Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Hare Hare / Hare Rāma Hare Rāma, Rāma Rāma Hare Hare* — bead by bead, all the way around (stopping at the head bead), then turning around and going back the other way. Once around is one **round;** we do **16 rounds**, which takes about two hours.
- "Two hours? Anything better you can do with your time? No." *Athāto brahma jijñāsā* — now that you've come to the human form, now is the time to go within and discover your spiritual nature. What's the harm in giving two hours, when the average American watches three and a half hours of television every day — mostly violence, sex, politics? This is deeper subject matter.

### The Holy Name
- This chanting is central to our practice. There are three names — **Hare, Kṛṣṇa, and Rāma** — different names of God in Sanskrit. The idea is that the Lord is present in the sound of His name. Your parents could have named you anything; your name is a label. But on the absolute platform, in the spiritual world, there is no difference between the object and its name — so whether you say Kṛṣṇa, Yahweh, Allah, or Buddha (any name of your tradition), the Lord is actually present in that sound vibration. Because He is the supreme pure, when you chant, you become purified.
- All the great teachings of the world stress chanting these mantras:
  - **Bṛhan-nāradīya Purāṇa:** *harer nāma harer nāma harer nāmaiva kevalam / kalau nāsty eva nāsty eva nāsty eva gatir anyathā.*
  - **The Bible, the Psalms of David** (recalled from being an altar boy): "From the rising of the sun until the setting of the same, the Lord's name is to be praised, with drums and cymbals."
  - **Buddha:** "Anyone who calls upon my name shall be delivered."
- These are universal truths flowing through every tradition; the only differences are the clothes, the language, and some of the rituals. It's senseless that religions fight, when they have one common goal.
- Because Kṛṣṇa is present in His name, and He is the personification of bliss and happiness as the Supreme Person, when you chant you experience the highest pleasure — and that is the only reason we can follow the strict discipline required of any transcendentalist to get out of the repetition of birth and death.

### The Four Regulative Principles
- (Usually mentioned at the end, because seekers are left at the end.) We follow four principles: **vegetarian** (no intoxication of any kind — no drugs, marijuana, liquor, beer, no smoking, not even coffee or tea), **no sex outside of marriage,** and **no gambling.**

So to achieve the highest goal of life — liberation from **saṁsāra**, the repetition of birth and death, and to go back to the spiritual world — expect to practice some discipline. It's not enough to read a book or go to your temple, church, or mosque once a week; it has to be a whole culture, a whole way of life. In ancient India this was integral to society; everybody was vegetarian.

But we deal with stress naturally; we transcend it, like the **lotus flower**, whose stem goes down into the mud but whose flower always stays a little above the water — technically not in the water. As the Bible says, be **"in this world but not of this world."** If your consciousness is transcendental — always thinking of God, offering the results of your activities to God — you're not stressed. Like the **swan:** pour milk into water and the swan drinks the milk but lets the water out of its beak. A transcendentalist, a yogī, a ṛṣi, is a **Paramahaṁsa** — a swan-like man who takes the essential things from life and leaves the rest.
- **On sex:** Sex is needed, otherwise there'd be no species — but in Vedic culture it was used only for procreation. That energy is very powerful, and the yogī conserves it. The semen can go downward or upward; yogīs remain celibate so it goes to the brain and nourishes the finer tissues for understanding philosophy — it has two purposes. Same for a woman. A man and woman unite as many times as they desire to have children, but children born to such an enlightened couple are very special — the speaker sees it traveling, especially in India, where couples reserve the act of love for procreating a spiritually elevated child, and those children are very advanced (like prodigy children in the West). We don't say "no sex," but it's meant for that purpose; used too much the other way, we see the problems today — unwanted children, broken families, sexual diseases. The easiest way to control the AIDS problem is celibacy; it's a tough one, not expected of everyone in the beginning, but it has its merits.
- **On gambling:** it means taking something valuable and throwing it to the wind; it becomes an addiction in itself.
- These principles (maybe not from the very beginning, but they can be followed) are necessary for transcending this world — and you can follow them if you're getting a **higher taste.** The taste from chanting this mantra is higher than any experience in this world. ("I've been chanting Hare Kṛṣṇa almost 40 years and never get tired of it.")
- **Analogy — the favorite song:** Your band's new CD comes out and there's a song you love — "this is it, our song" — but after a few weeks you move on to another group; you can't keep singing it for years. But mantra meditation, because it's a divine sound from an eternal realm full of knowledge and bliss (unlike this temporary world full of ignorance and suffering), is different. That real world beyond the material creation does exist; real people come from there at different times in history with basically the same message. The happiness from that world is **ānanda** — not decreasing but ever-increasing. As soon as you enjoy something here it begins decreasing, and after 15 minutes or half an hour it's finished; there, it's always increasing. As a small drop of water gives an idea of the whole ocean, through this chanting you get an idea of being back in the spiritual world — so much pleasure that you never get tired of it. Every time you chant it's fresh, like the first time — that's the nature of the spiritual energy.
- You can take this home — this is not an ordinary program like a disco or a mere lecture; there's a real experience here, something very deep, and you can take it home by chanting. **"The proof of the pudding is in the eating."** You don't have to dress like this or shave your head.

## Questions and Answers — Second Round

### Q: Plants also have life — how does eating them compare with eating animals?
- It's not enough to be vegetarian; you have to take it one step further — **prasādam.** Prasādam means food authorized by the Lord in our scriptures, offered to Him first as a thanks before we eat.
- **BG 9.26** (paraphrased): Kṛṣṇa says, "Offer Me with love a fruit, a flower, or a leaf — these things with devotion I will accept." There is no mention of meat, fish, or eggs.
- Yes, you take life when you eat plants — but you have to eat something, and the idea is to **keep the violence to a minimum.** Even walking, cooking, or mowing the lawn you are killing; the idea is to minimize it. The pain experienced by a plant is much less than that of a more evolved species (a sheep, cow, or horse); picking a fruit or harvesting grain doesn't cause the pain and trauma a slaughterhouse does. Still, you can take that life because you have to eat, and what you eat is authorized by He who creates life.
- **Genesis:** God says, "I give you the seed-bearing fruit, and for you it shall be meat" — an authorization to eat vegetarian food. (After the flood there was an emergency, so there are also injunctions in the Bible for eating meat, as there are in the Vedic scriptures, but mostly in times of emergency.)
- **The government analogy:** Sometimes the government authorizes killing and sometimes it doesn't. We send our boys to Iraq (not supporting the war) — the government authorizes them to kill to protect the citizens, and they get a medal of honor; the same boy comes back and shoots indiscriminately in New York City, and the same government puts him in jail. Likewise, God authorizes the eating of certain foods and not others — the plant kingdom, but not more evolved species.
- So you take the vegetables, prepare them, and as a vote of thanks put them on the altar and offer them to Kṛṣṇa first. He accepts the offering through our prayers, and the food is sanctified. In India, from the temple you get a little piece of **mahā-prasādam** from the Deity's plate, taken with great reverence — not just tasty food, but blessed; eating it, your consciousness is purified.
- Conversely, eating meat degrades the body because you lose **compassion**, one of the most important qualities of human life. When human beings lose compassion for others, they descend to an animal existence. If people had to go to the slaughterhouse and kill the animal themselves, how many could do it? (In France, until a few years ago, a butcher couldn't sit on a jury, as he was felt to lack the discretion or compassion.)
- **Karma:** We are responsible for what we do. Karma means for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction — do good, good comes back; do bad, bad comes back. It's like physics: by a certain force you can predict how far an object will go. It's a universal law the scientists haven't yet discovered, and everybody is controlled by it whether they know it or not (driving 120 in a 40 zone, "I didn't know, sir" — it's your responsibility to know). Meat-eating is bad by the universal law of karma; somewhere down the road you'll get it. That's why bad things happen to good people — somewhere previously they did something.
- **Story — the Australian aborigines and the boomerang:** Recently in the Australian outback, among aborigines still living in the wild (nice people, in some ways more God conscious than many, living close to nature — "man created the city but God created the country"), the speaker tried to explain the law of karma but couldn't get it across. There was a boomerang on the ground (which he'd liked to throw as a kid); he picked it up, threw it, and it came right back. They were impressed ("You know boomerang?"). "That's karma — what goes around comes around. You throw it and it comes back; life's like that." They caught it. "So you shouldn't eat meat." "But we have to eat lizards and deer." "Move into the city; cultivate the land." A hard one.
- **Story — the yogī's four benedictions:** In India, sādhus, ṛṣis, and yogīs give benedictions (like the Pope giving blessings). One yogī visited a town and blessed four people differently:
  - To **the prince:** "May you live a long time" — because as a prince he obviously had good karma from a previous pious life (charity, helping people), which he was now reaping. But no one has all good or all bad karma, so: live as long as you can, because soon your bad karma will catch up in your next life.
  - To **the monk** (practicing so many penances, austerities, and study of scripture): "May you die immediately" — because in your next life you'll reap the result of all this austerity.
  - To **the saintly person:** "Live or die, it doesn't make any difference" — because by his practice of yoga he is already transcendental; Kṛṣṇa conscious now, Kṛṣṇa conscious in his next life, death doesn't disturb him.
  - To **the butcher:** "Don't live, don't die" — don't live, because every day, killing so many innocent animals just for the taste buds, he's digging his own grave; and don't die, because when he dies he'll get all the results of that bad karma. So, in a sense, suspended animation.
- There is some karma even in eating plants, but if you follow the Gītā and offer the food first, you become absolved even of that — what you're eating now is a **karma-free diet.** ("You could eat this food the rest of your life and get no karma from your food" — and a lot of our karma comes from our food, since we eat two or three times a day.)
- This is nothing new in Indian culture, but in the past couple of hundred years meat-eating has become popular — even in Bombay and Delhi there are McDonald's and Wendy's, and people think nothing of it. It's one thing if you don't know, but if your culture, your forefathers, your **gotra** (lineage going back to a great sage like Vasiṣṭha Muni) tell you not to, you know better. Because of Western influence, India is giving it up and thinking Sītā, Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa, and Hanumān are mythology, and that the Gītā is just an analogy (the five Pāṇḍavas as the five senses, Kṛṣṇa as the mind). But this is a real culture, those persons are real, and the philosophy is real — Śrīla Vyāsadeva wasn't wasting his time writing fairy tales.
- For the West it's a positive alternative, because in one sense Western culture is doomed ("we're not doomsday philosophers"): global warming, the melting ice caps, polluted air and water. You can't turn a blind eye. It's not enough to sort the paper, plastic, and glass or use ethanol — it requires a whole change of civilization; not just a change of habit but a **change of heart** — going back to a simpler, more spiritual existence, conserving energy and taking only what we need.
- **BG 4.7** (*yadā yadā hi dharmasya glānir bhavati bhārata...*): "Whenever and wherever there is a decline in spirituality (dharma) and an increase in materialism, at that time I descend, millennium after millennium." The Gītā was spoken 5,000 years ago, but Kṛṣṇa says He spoke it to the sun-god **Vivasvān** millions of years ago (**BG 4.1**). History repeats in cycles: people are spiritual for a while, then lose the plot and become material, then something happens and they become spiritual again. So Kṛṣṇa comes age after age to establish **dharma** — a sensible, sane way of life where we can meet our material needs and at the same time achieve liberation from the cycle of birth and death. Different representatives have come from the spiritual world at different times, which is why there are different religions.
- **"One God, so why different religions?"** As there is one science of mathematics, why are there different schools and levels? Because some people want a little, some more, some a lot. Study the Gītā and you'll see there's a lot there — which doesn't mean its followers are better (a PhD student isn't better than a grade-schooler), but if you really want to learn a lot about spiritual life, the Gītā and its lifestyle are there to realize that knowledge. That's why we have temples all over the world.

### Q: On whether the *way* you eat (with love), or the fact that plants and animals both have souls, makes a difference
- You have to eat something, so it lies with the **authority** to decide what you can and cannot eat. (Again the government analogy: killing on the battlefield in a defensive war, to protect the citizens against an aggressor, is authorized; taking the same gun and indiscriminately killing in the town is not. It's within the government's jurisdiction to decide.) Because God gives life and God takes life, it's within His jurisdiction to say what we can and cannot eat, and for reasons best known to Him He allows us to take life from lower down the evolutionary scale — the plants.
- One thing to note: when a plant is offered to Kṛṣṇa specifically, that soul makes incredible spiritual advancement. That's not an excuse for eating plants, but that plant makes a lot of advancement itself. Still, eating the plant is the lesser of two evils — less pain and trauma than slaughtering a cow, deer, or sheep.

### Q: What about cockroaches (and pests)?
- We don't exterminate them. That situation generally comes about through **uncleanliness** — fleas, mice, rats, and cockroaches are attracted to an unclean environment. **Cleanliness** is central to Vedic culture. As the spiritual master said, "Cleanliness means a place for everything and everything in its place" — not just wiping off the kitchen sink.
- In India a brāhmaṇa lives by certain principles — **cleanliness, truthfulness, mercy, and austerity.** A brāhmaṇa is himself very clean (two or three baths a day, changing clothes maybe twice a day), his house is clean and orderly, and his heart is clean because he is always cleansing it with mantra, with transcendental sound. If the area is really clean, these animals don't accumulate and you don't have a pest problem.
- If you move into a place infested with termites, that's another thing — but we probably wouldn't move into a situation where we'd have to compromise our principles by killing all the pests.
- However, **one can kill an aggressor.** Vedic culture advocates **ahiṁsā** (non-violence), but even so, if someone is an aggressor attacking you with deadly weapons, you are allowed to defend yourself. If someone is going to rape your daughter, you don't use Gandhi's philosophy of non-cooperation — that wasn't the philosophy of the Gītā, where Kṛṣṇa told Arjuna to fight to protect the innocent; that's dharma. Likewise, in an area infested with mosquitoes carrying malaria or yellow fever (like the Congo), the mosquito is an aggressor — you can use the spray, and if you're getting bitten you can tap him, because you may die otherwise. ("I got malaria because I wasn't careful enough — a horrible disease that never really goes away; it stays dormant and comes out again.") The tiger is ultimately your brother, but you don't open your arms to "my brother the tiger" like Saint Francis (who said "my brother deer, my sister mouse" — thought crazy, but he had the vision of the true equality of all spirit souls; he was himself vegetarian, but surely if a bear attacked he would defend himself). Man's glory also lies in his common sense.

### Q: On the cycle of inaction — seeing an opportunity to act but not acting, and getting stuck
- This is like **lethargy** — another symptom of which is to put off for tomorrow what you can do today. It's a problem in material life and in spiritual life: one becomes conditioned to his religion or tradition, gets a sense of wellness, and doesn't push forward to get "the gold medal" — so he doesn't achieve perfection.
- Therefore, in Vedic culture, one has a spiritual master who is always encouraging the disciple to move on and go forward — like a **coach.** ("As a kid in football I thought I was doing pretty good, but my coach saw more in me than I knew I had, and was always pushing us — extra hours of practice — so our team was always winning; without that coach I couldn't have done it.") In spiritual life we can lose many opportunities just because we feel good and think we're doing all right — so we need a coach, the spiritual master, whose duty is not just to bless the disciple but to find out his weakness, what's keeping him attached to this world.
- **Story — the guru holding the tree:** An arrogant student came to his guru: "Guruji, I'm not giving up my material desires because it's not really me — it's just the material energy that has a hold on me and won't let go." The guru got angry, took him along, put his arms around a tree, and started screaming, "Tree, let me go! Let me go, tree!" The student said, "Guru, the tree is not holding onto you — you're holding onto the tree. Just let go." The guru turned around: "Yes — in the same way, you're holding onto your material desires; the material energy is not holding onto you. Just let go." Chastised with this heavy lesson, the disciple gradually gave up his material desires. It is said that the spiritual master, more often than not, chastises his disciple rather than blesses him, until the disciple reaches a certain stage of perfection — always on his case (in modern language) to get moving and make spiritual progress.

### Q: What if you couldn't have a spiritual "coach" and had to deal with the fear yourself?
- That's very difficult. Most of what we learn in life we learn from others. If you could put all your knowledge on the table, you'd find that most of what you know you actually learned from others (parents, friends, schoolteachers, previous philosophers, scientists, music teachers) — only a very small amount you learned yourself. So it makes more sense to accept a teacher.
- **The boy who wanted no one between him and God:** He said, "If there's God and I'm a child of God, I don't need somebody blocking the way." No — it's like the **moon and the mirror:** the teacher takes the reflection of the distant moon in a mirror and brings it very close to you. Or like a **telescope:** the guru is between you and the moon, but through the telescope everything is magnified so much that it's almost as if you can touch the moon and see its craters. The spiritual teacher is not meant to be a block between you and the Supreme, but to facilitate your relationship with Him — teaching you more about what the Supreme is like, His personality and nature, and your nature, and how you can relate together in a very intimate, loving way.
- We need help. Trying to do it yourself, you may stumble across some truth, but there's always the risk of a mistake, because to err is human. We have **certain inherent faults:** we make mistakes, our senses are imperfect, we have a tendency to cheat, and we have a tendency to fall under illusion (the four defects). These make it impossible for us to understand the Absolute Truth on our own; so we need an ambassador from the spiritual realm to teach us how to achieve perfection.
- **The Ādi Guru:** The original guru (Sanskrit *Ādi Guru*) is God, and God is not subject to the inebrieties of material existence — any more than the government is subject to the frailties of the prison it creates. (The prison isn't like the real world; it's made to reform you so that you come out a good citizen. Similarly, God creates this world — not in a sadistic way, but as a place to reform us — where we can try to act out being God, the supreme enjoyer and master, but all our plans fall short.) After many lifetimes we turn back to God, but because He is so far away and we're so unqualified to associate with Him, He sends the guru, prophet, or messiah — a person like a **lawyer** with power of attorney, an empowered representative. His qualification is not that he says something cute or new that he made up, but that he **repeats the same knowledge of God** in a way the people of that time and history can understand. Take the teachings of Muhammad, Jesus, Buddha, or Kṛṣṇa — they're all the same, given according to the time and people. They are authorized representatives, but the original teacher is God, and the real spiritual master is one who represents Him. That is the missing link.

### Q: What happens to the ātmā when someone attains supreme enlightenment?
- When the soul attains enlightenment, first he realizes **who he is** as a spiritual person, then he realizes **God**, and then he realizes his **intimate relationship with God.**
- In the West we believe in God — a step in the right direction — but ask anyone who God is, and it's difficult ("He's a person, He's there"). That's pretty meager knowledge, and it's difficult to really love someone you don't know much about. When you love someone, you love them specifically — the way they look, talk, walk, and speak, their particular interests (sailing, cards). The impetus of love is the detail of the personality. Without actually knowing who God is, it's hard to really love Him; you can have a sentiment of allegiance, but real love means knowing who they are.
- The beauty of the Vedic scriptures is that there is a very detailed description of God, His abode, the personalities who live there, and the relationships between them and God. This is a deep subject called **rasa** — the different relationships you can have with God. Basically all the loving relationships we have in this world can also be had with God, because the relationships are here since they are there — this world is a **perverted reflection** of the spiritual world.
- Here we love someone as our friend, our master, our child, and our lover. In the spiritual world God, though so great, allows Himself to have those relationships with His devotees — just as a president, when he gets home, plays "horsey" with his kids, coming down to their level, because that's the nature of love. God's greatness is that He allows His devotees to relate to Him as the best friend, the lover, as their child, or as their master. So when you become self-realized, you realize your relationship with God — as a friend, a lover, in a parental relationship, or as master and servant. This is not revealed in Western scriptures, which see God only as the supreme master; but there is more love between friends than between master and servant, more love between a mother and child, and more love between a lover and beloved — so the pinnacle of all love is to love God as your lover. It is nothing mundane or sexual — just the mood that you love God more than anyone else in the whole world.
- To become self-realized doesn't mean the lights just go on and you see a brilliant light everywhere, or that you merge into God/the light (as some schools say), or become void (as in Buddhism — free from desire, and thus from suffering). No — when you become self-realized you become very **active** in the spiritual world, in a very intimate, loving relationship with God. It's a deep explanation, not everyone will understand it, but it's simple and logical: we love God, love means relationship, and there are many types of loving relationships with God. So self-realization means going out of this world back to the spiritual world, where you associate with God in various ways.
- **Love is the essence, the main thing in life.** The best kind of happiness is love. If you have everything but no love, you're miserable; if you have love and nothing, the two of you can be happy because you have each other. But love in this world is temporary — Romeo and Juliet were the perfect couple who never considered divorce, but even the most perfect relationship isn't walking off into the sunset to live happily ever after; that's sentiment. **BG 2.27** (paraphrased): "For one who takes birth, death is certain; and for one who dies, birth is certain." These are the hard, cold, axiomatic facts of life, and nothing will change them. Realizing that, we should want an alternative — as **Buddha** did: born a prince, when he realized what this world was really like (people dying, getting sick), he told his father "you created a utopia I'm not interested in," and sat under the famous tree and became enlightened. We shouldn't become depressed, but should use the temporary, miserable nature of this world as a stepping-stone toward enlightenment, wanting a loving relationship with no beginning and no end. The only way is to go back to the spiritual world, where birth, disease, old age, and death are conspicuous by their absence, and where everything we're looking for exists in perfection. You need a little faith that that place exists — and you get that faith by associating with people who have that realization, and by practicing a spiritual way of life, until you come to it yourself. Then, Kṛṣṇa says, when you die you go to that world and never take birth again.

### Q: You said the ātmā is unchangeable — so what does this world actually do to it, and what does the soul "do" to go back and associate with God?
- **Unchangeable** means, as Kṛṣṇa says in the Gītā (**BG 2.23**), the soul cannot be burnt by fire, withered by the wind, or drowned by water — it is unaffected, changeless, ever fresh and ever the same.
- But because the soul is **so small** (God is very great, the soul very small), unlike God — who never comes under illusion because He is so great and the controller of all energies — we are tiny. We are like God by nature/quality but not in quantity, like a spark from the fire that is like the fire but very small. Because we're so small, we can become **covered** by the powerful material illusion: though we can't be burnt, withered, or drowned, and can't be touched by this world's suffering directly, we become overcome by illusion and **identify with what is material**, and therefore we suffer.
- **The car again:** You're not the car, but if your car is looking good you feel good, because you identify with it; and if someone smashes it with a hammer you're ready to have a heart attack, because you identify so strongly. The soul is changeless and transcendental at all times, but because it's so small and becomes covered by material illusion, it identifies with the body — so much so that if the body looks good he thinks "I'm looking good today," and if it has a blemish or is fat he feels depressed. It's all misidentification.
- By hearing from a spiritual master week after week, month after month, year after year, you begin to get the point: "I am not the body, so I shouldn't act just as a body — I should act as the driver, as the soul, and do the things the soul does." And what does the soul do? **The soul serves God.** How do you serve God? Whatever you do in this world, you offer it to God in an act of love instead of using it for selfish purposes — and thereby you become **jīvan-mukta** (liberated even while living in this world). You've changed so much that you're no longer a creature of this world; and when this body dies, you naturally go back to the spiritual world, just as water seeks its own level. A liberated soul at death has no material desires (so no reason to come back) and such evolved, matured spiritual desires (because of the way he lived) that he qualifies to go back and associate with God in a spiritual body. God has a spiritual body and form; you have a spiritual body and form; and in those forms you associate with God — as we associate in this world, but not in a selfish, material way, rather in a very intimate, spiritual, loving way.

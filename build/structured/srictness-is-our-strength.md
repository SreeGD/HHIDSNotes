## The Verse: Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 5.18.13

- Reading from **Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam, Canto 5, Chapter 18 — "The Residents of Jambūdvīpa Offer Prayers," text 13.**
- **SB 5.18.13** (*ādir hi bhagavān...*): "Just as aquatics always desire to remain in the vast mass of water, all conditioned living entities naturally desire to remain in the vast existence of the Supreme Lord. Therefore, someone very great by material calculations fails to take shelter of the Supreme Soul, but instead becomes attached to material household life. His greatness is like that of a young, low-class couple. One who is too attached to material life loses all good spiritual qualities."
- Word-for-word highlights: *ādi* — He (the origin); *sākṣāt bhagavān* — directly the Supreme Personality of Godhead; *śarīriṇām* — of all embodied living entities; *ātmā* — the life and soul; *jhaṣāṇām iva toyam* — like the vast water desired by the aquatics; *hitvā* — giving up; *mahāntam* — the great personality; *gṛhe sajjate* — becomes attached to household life; *tan-mahatvam* — his greatness; is compared to that of *vayasā dampatīnām* — a young husband and wife.

### Prabhupāda's Purport — the Crocodile Out of Water
- Although crocodiles are very fierce animals, they are powerless when they venture out of the water onto land; there they cannot exhibit their original power.
- Similarly, the all-pervading Supersoul, Paramātmā, is the source of all living entities, who are part and parcel of Him. When the living entity remains in contact with all-pervading Vāsudeva, he manifests his spiritual power, exactly as the crocodile exhibits its strength in the water.
- The greatness of the living entity can be perceived **when he is in the spiritual world, engaged in spiritual activities.**
- Many householders, though well educated in Vedic knowledge, become attached to family life; they are compared to crocodiles out of water — devoid of all spiritual strength. Their greatness is like that of a young husband and wife who, though uneducated, praise one another and become attracted to their own temporary beauty — a greatness appreciated only by low-class men with no qualifications.
- Everyone should seek the shelter of the Supreme Soul. In Vedic civilization this "crippled" household life is allowed only until one's fiftieth year, when one must take **vānaprastha** (retired life for cultivating spiritual knowledge) or **sannyāsa** (complete shelter of the Supreme Lord).

## The Living Entity Is Glorious Only in Relationship with the Lord

- Prabhupāda states the greatness of the living entity is perceived when he is in the spiritual world engaged in spiritual activities.
- **"Jīvera 'svarūpa' haya — kṛṣṇera 'nitya-dāsa'"** (*Caitanya-caritāmṛta*) — the eternal nature of the living entity is that he is the eternal servant of God. Situated in that eternal relationship as servant, his real glory comes out. But if the servant leaves the master, he can only become a beggar; his opulence and good fortune exist because he is serving his master.

### Story — The Two Kinds of Dogs on Bombay Beach
- Prabhupāda was walking on the beach in Bombay. Some dogs ran free; others were on a leash, attached to a master.
- Prabhupāda pointed out the difference: the fat, fluffy dogs are like that because they have a master who maintains and cares for them; those with no master, or who have run away, are skinny and weak because they must survive on their own.
- So too, the living entity connected to the Supreme Personality of Godhead possesses all good qualities; as soon as he leaves the association of the Lord who maintains him, he is bereft of all good qualities, struggling on his own.

### Analogy — The Wilted Flower
- On a drive down by Mission Bay, looking at the Ratha-yātrā site, there was a beautiful peach tree in full spring bloom. Indradyumna Swami asked Bhāva Prabhu to pick some flowers to offer to Lord Nṛsiṁhadeva.
- Halfway back he realized they could not be offered — they were all wilted. The flowers were beautiful only in relationship to the source of their beauty, the tree; the flower was drawing its life from the main trunk, and as soon as it was picked it began to wither and die.
- We are part and parcel of Kṛṣṇa; our eternal constitutional position is that of servant. To be the servant of the Supreme Lord is very glorious — but when we foolishly give up that service, we become insignificant beggars.

### Analogy — The Harmonium Screw
- A harmonium has many essential parts. A single screw is very important. But if that screw falls out on the ground, it is insignificant — just a screw, throw it away. It is insignificant only because it is out of relationship with its position in the harmonium. Put it back in its place and it is very much appreciated.
- The living entity is appreciated in his service to God; as soon as he leaves the service of God he becomes ugly.

### Analogy — The Thumb, Gandhi, and the British
- The thumb is a very important part of the body; we don't realize how much we use it.
- During British rule, Indians wanted **swarāj** (independence) and sought self-sufficiency. Gandhi began making their own cloth rather than selling wool to the British, who would take it to London, make fine clothes, and sell them back to the Indians at very high prices (it was fashionable to wear something "made in London") — thereby cheating the people with their own product.
- Gandhi said no cooperation with the British and started spinning mills; the people spun their own cloth. This agitated the British, who were losing business.
- So the British went to the mill workers and **cut off their thumbs** — since spinning yarn is simple with the thumb but very hard without it.
- The thumb is very important in relationship to the hand; but as soon as it is cut off and lies on the ground, it becomes a disgusting thing — pick it up, throw it away.

### Story — The Woodshop Accident
- Once in school woodshop, working with a buzzsaw making models and boats, the boy next to Indradyumna — one of the football stars, with a "good hand" — lost his finger.
- Fortunately they picked up the finger, took him to the hospital, and sewed it back on. The only thing he couldn't do afterward was play the violin (the tendons didn't all work).
- Everyone appreciated the boy's good hand — his "golden finger." But once separated from the whole (the body), it became ugly.
- So the living entity is glorious in his relationship with the Supreme Lord as His servant; separated from God, he becomes ugly. But if you rejoin the finger to the body, the flower to the tree, or the living entity to God through the constitutional position of devotional service, those good qualities come out again.

## All Good Qualities Come from Devotion (Association)

- **SB 5.18.12** (*yasyāsti bhaktir bhagavaty akiñcanā sarvair guṇais tatra samāsate surāḥ* — yesterday's verse): One who is a devotee of the Lord naturally develops all good qualities. Those who are not devotees possess no good qualities, because they hover on the mental platform and are sure to be attracted by the glaring material energy.
- We are the **marginal potency** of the Lord and take on the qualities of whatever we associate with. The spiritual world is all-auspicious; the material world is inauspicious. Associate materially and we take on the bad qualities. It is all a question of association.
- Link up to the Supreme Lord through service and — as with the 26 qualities discussed yesterday — we do not have to develop each quality individually; simply by being a devotee those qualities become manifest in one's personality. We simply concentrate on Kṛṣṇa consciousness, the process of devotional service.

## To Serve Is Our Constitutional Nature
- To serve is natural — that is our constitutional position. People say, "Serve God? I don't want to serve God." In France they say **"ni maître ni dieu"** (no master, no God). But still they must serve — country, family, society.
- Prabhupāda: if someone lacks a personal relationship of service, he'll get a cat, a dog, or a bird — that relationship is needed and can never be separated from the living entity. It is the intrinsic nature, the **dharma** of the soul: as sugar is always sweet and salt always salty, the nature of the soul is always to serve.
- If we're not serving God we serve something else — as the Christians say, we serve **mammon**, we serve matter. But serve you must.
- The taste one gets from service is **rasa**. In the material world this is incomplete: we try to serve separate from God — society, friendship, love — but the taste doesn't come and we remain unfulfilled.
- As soon as we take up devotional service through the **nine processes** (śravaṇaṁ, kīrtanaṁ, viṣṇoḥ smaraṇaṁ, pāda-sevanaṁ, arcanaṁ, etc.), we immediately feel fulfilled — like a man taking a meal who perceives himself becoming satisfied — because we are again connected to the Source. Cut off from the Source, nutrition stops; reconnected, life comes back to the living entity.

### Story — The Triple Bypass Operation
- In France, a devotee's father had a triple bypass operation. Visiting him in the hospital, he told this amazing story: they cut open the chest, open the ribcage, and actually take the heart some distance out of the body to work on it.
- Because the heart is no longer pumping blood and oxygen — its essential function — the body is technically considered dead (no source). So they hook it to a life-support system: a machine pumps the blood, another works the lungs, and if necessary a kidney machine keeps the kidneys going.
- It looks like the person is functioning and living, but it is all artificial — not the real system of life, just an artificial system of support.
- Similarly, the life materialistic people experience is not real life. Real life is devotional service — spiritual life. Because they hover on the mental platform and are attracted to the material energy, it is like a **"dead man walking"**: their source of inspiration is matter, which is by nature inert and dead. But spirit is life, the soul is actually living. Real life is Kṛṣṇa consciousness. That is why devotees are effulgent and full of transcendental energy — all good qualities become manifest by connecting to the original Source, the Supreme Personality of Godhead.

## The Strict Warning of the Verse — Strength Within, Weakness Without

- The verse gives a strict warning: remain in the association of the Lord (devotional service) and you exhibit your original power — you are strong. Outside that association you become defeated, weakened by the all-powerful material energy.
- **The crocodile example (Prabhupāda's):** the crocodile is powerful in water, with his big tail that can drag you into the water and even eat you. But on land it is awkward — his little stubby legs and his long tail (a propeller in water) are useless and just get in the way. He cannot fight on land.

### The Story of Gajendra
- In the Eighth Canto, Gajendra the elephant. The elephant is very powerful; though the lion is called king of the jungle, elephants can actually defeat lions. But put that big elephant in the water and he becomes incompetent — the crocodile can attack him there.
- In the purports to that pastime, Prabhupāda says **the devotee must learn the art of situating himself so that he always remains strong in Kṛṣṇa consciousness.** In the spiritual world one has all good qualities and exhibits his original power; one cannot be defeated by the material energy there. But coming into the material world is an awkward situation — like the elephant in water or the crocodile on land — and that powerful living entity can be defeated.
- Though we are spirit soul, part and parcel of Kṛṣṇa, possessing all the divine qualities of the Lord in a minute form, being the marginal potency we can be overcome by the material energy. But if we act in the same consciousness here — through devotional service — we remain protected. We have to find that situation.
- Prabhupāda: for some it may be **brahmacārī**, for some **gṛhastha**, for others **sannyāsa**. Wherever one feels strong, wherever one gets spiritual strength, one should situate himself in that particular service, that varṇa, that āśrama, where he can perform devotional service and not be agitated by the modes of material nature.

### Maximum Security — Bhagavad-gītā 7.14
- **BG 7.14** (*daivī hy eṣā guṇamayī mama māyā duratyayā, mām eva ye prapadyante māyām etāṁ taranti te*): "This divine energy of Mine, consisting of the three modes of material nature, is very difficult to overcome. But those who have surrendered unto Me can easily overcome it."
- This material world is **maximum security.** As an especially bad criminal — a rapist, a "mother-stabber," a "father-strangler" — is put in maximum security so he can't get out: seven walls, a moat full of crocodiles, an electric fence, poisonous gas, guards. It doesn't matter how "good" a criminal you are, you can't get out.
- Some people have escaped even maximum security — but there is no way out of the kingdom of Durgā, of Māyā. This is Māyā's kingdom; she has the thankless task of prison-keeper. The prison's four walls are **birth, death, old age, and disease.** Very difficult to get out.
- But Kṛṣṇa says those who have surrendered unto Him can easily cross beyond it — and the surrendering process is devotional service.

## The Humiliation of the Materialist

- The glorious spirit soul who exists in his full glory in the spiritual world has now become a pig or a dog, attracted to stool.
- **India's stool fields:** we may think a toilet is more civilized, but Vedic culture was perfect — people would go to the fields, it was clean, and the sun would dry everything (the sewage system was introduced later). On early-morning parikramas, people answering nature's call in the fields would be met by a whole army of hogs, sometimes so eager they'd knock people over.
- It's humorous — like when Kṛṣṇa, seeing the despondent Arjuna surrendered unto Him, began to instruct him: **BG 2.10** — "The Lord, gently smiling (*prahasann iva*), began to instruct." But consider: that pig or hog was originally in his glory in the spiritual world, and by the powerful material energy he is now convinced that chasing after stool is his breakfast, something very nice. We are humiliated, defeated; all our good qualities are taken away.
- **Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura:** this materialistic way of life makes one look more foolish than an ass.
- Even one who becomes very great by material calculations, if he fails to take shelter of the Supreme Lord and instead becomes attached to household life, his greatness is like that of a young, low-class couple; too attached, he loses all good qualities. His greatness is only momentary because he will be defeated by the material energy.

### Old Age and Sickness Humiliate the Bodily Conception
- Old age and sickness are very humiliating for a materialist, who prides himself on beauty, strength, and education. That pretty little face with pearl-like teeth, ruby-like eyes, and soft smooth skin slowly starts, and pretty soon you look like a prune — very embarrassing.
- The karmīs have only a few short years of so-called glory (16 to 21). Up to about 24 the body is developing — the **yauvanam**, the freshness of youth. Around 24 it starts to deteriorate. By 40 you've got the extra tire on the side, lines under the eyes, teeth turning yellow, suddenly you can't digest the same Sunday feasts, and the hair starts turning gray.
- On the bodily platform you get discouraged. But the devotee says, "Great — getting closer to Kṛṣṇa. These powerful senses are becoming weakened; they won't demand so much anymore. I can get down to my real business of practicing Kṛṣṇa consciousness." Everything is reversed. Old age and disease don't bother devotees; they are transcendental because they have spiritual knowledge. But the materialist, attached to the bodily concept, finds that the very qualities that made him great will humiliate him.

### Examples — Johnny Weissmuller and Elvis Presley
- **Johnny Weissmuller** — a star Olympic swimmer in America, the original Tarzan in the movies. ("I wish I could use more spiritual examples — it brings it home.") With his big strong body he enjoyed great fame and honor. But that same body that was the source of his fame, prestige, and pride became the source of his anxiety. As he got older, flabby and fat (with money, eating junk food), he was no longer attractive, became so frustrated that he went a little mad — it is described that he actually went insane.
- **Elvis Presley** — the same: very famous, then became fat, and when he could no longer sing, killed himself with an overdose.

## Never Put Faith in the Material Energy
- Prabhupāda: material life means "you put your head on my lap and I cut your throat."
- We respect Mother Durgā — "you are the powerful agent of the Supreme Lord" — but we want to be Kṛṣṇa's servant, because if we serve her she is a merciless master: she promises name, fame, good fortune, gives it for a while, and in the end cuts our throat; those very things turn sour.

### Analogy — The Soured Sweet Rice
- Sweet rice is very relishable, but if you leave it out in the hot sun (in the month of January — southern hemisphere) and come drink it the next day, you'll get sick.
- A Sunday-feast example: someone made the sweet rice on Friday, it was served with "a little tingle," and by Monday morning nobody came to Maṅgala-ārati — everyone was running to the restroom. Material life is like that; it has both sides.

### Analogy — The Two Theater Masks
- The theater of life is represented by the happy face and the sad face — a tradition (e.g., at the Paris Opéra, which he used to distribute in front of). The realization came: this is material life. You can't have one without the other; that smiling face will eventually turn into the sad face.
- Happiness and distress, heat and cold, good and bad — the duality of materialistic life. If we accept the so-called happiness of material existence, in due course of time we get an equal amount of distress. Invest in a big attractive house — "I made it" — and you also have big anxiety, because many people are envious and want to steal your things.
- **South Africa:** two out of five houses are burglarized. Ask someone at a home program, "Has your house been burgled?" "Oh yes, three times." Success is nice, but you have to board it up, lock it, keep a dog, build a fence — a source of happiness, but an equal weight of distress.

### Story — Aṅgirā Muni's Benediction to the King
- Aṅgirā Muni gave a benediction to a king: "Yes, you can have a child; he will be the source of your happiness — and of your distress as well."
- The young, attractive prince was born, the life and soul of the king and queen. But out of envy the other queens poisoned the child, and that child became a source of great anxiety and distress.
- When the material energy is used for personal sense gratification, it has this effect. But a devotee sees the material as the property of Kṛṣṇa, so it does not have the same effect — the same thing that was causing the disease, used therapeutically, cures the disease.
- **Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī:** "I would have a sex life a thousand times if I could guarantee that those children would be Kṛṣṇa conscious."

### The Knot in the Heart — and Real Renunciation
- **SB 5.5.8** (*puṁsaḥ striyā mithunī-bhāvam etaṁ... hṛdaya-granthim āhur... aho... yam aham mameti*): The attraction between male and female is the basic principle of material existence; this misconception ties together the hearts of male and female. One becomes attached to his body, home, wife, children, and household, increasing life's illusions and thinking in terms of "I" and "mine." But the same principle used in Kṛṣṇa's service liberates.
- **Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura** had a big family (some 10 or 15 children), and they were all engaged — wife engaged, sons engaged (Lalitā Prasāda and Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī). He said, "One day I looked out and saw my house transformed into Vaikuṇṭha."
- Real renunciation: the Māyāvādīs also see the source of distress in the material world, but real renunciation does not mean simply to give it up — it never belonged to you anyway, so how can you give it up? Real renunciation means to see: this is not for my pleasure, this is for Kṛṣṇa's pleasure. Use the material energy in Kṛṣṇa's service and that which was causing you anxiety and distress becomes the very source of your liberation.

## The Glory of a Devotee's Old Age

- We must be intelligent to see how the material world binds the living entity, and how one who becomes great by the materialistic point of view will in due course of time be defeated.
- Old age is very humiliating for the materialist — he can only remember what it was like in the old days; the body old and useless, the mind not working. Nowadays people put the old away in rest homes because they've become senile, like babbling children again.
- But a devotee utilizes his life properly, does not exploit the body, uses the senses properly — so his glorious time is old age.

### Senility, Celibacy, and the Semen
- **Senility is a result of illicit sex life** (both gross and subtle), Prabhupāda said. But because devotees don't abuse this principle, having children only in Kṛṣṇa consciousness — which is religious, not an abuse — and because they control the senses, old age comes and they are in the prime of life.
- Brahmacārīs, and gṛhasthas who observe the principle (sex only for the propagation of children), find the semen goes **upwards.** The semen has two purposes; its basic purpose is to give strength to the brain. Yogīs, through the kuṇḍalinī process, raise the semen up — just as milk has the propensity to give finer brain cells so one can think of philosophy. The semen is one of the essential elements of the body (like the blood), meant to go upwards, giving strength to the mind to understand philosophy.
- **Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī** was a *naiṣṭhika-brahmacārī* from birth, never having a relationship with a woman. He was a **śruti-dhara** — he could remember any passage of any book he had read in his entire life. At seven years old he memorized the whole *Bhagavad-gītā*, every single verse ("we're still struggling — *dehino 'smin yathā dehe...* — **BG 2.13**"). And how he learned English: he was a Bengali speaker, so he took some encyclopedias, dictionaries, and technical books, locked himself in a room for five days, and came out speaking fluent, erudite English — better than any British person — so that he could preach.
- There is great strength in celibacy. All the "don'ts" have a definite reason. Freed from attraction for the opposite sex, one has so much energy. Not only does the semen go to the brain, but **subtle sex life** — profit, adoration, and distinction — is avoided. To achieve one's desire to enjoy the opposite sex, one builds a whole phantasmagoria of profit, adoration, and distinction to attract — like the male peacock doing his dance. So much endeavor, ambition, and distinction for a momentary pleasure; one works hard his whole life for a few minutes of enjoyment, then takes the risk.
- For a devotee, having children is a burden of love, a responsibility to raise them in Kṛṣṇa consciousness. But ordinary people have children like cats and dogs — a few moments' pleasure, then 25 or 30 years of paying the bills, from the moment of delivery until the child graduates from college.
- Even **Gandhi** — not such a glorious person, and one could say many things about him — was able to drive the British out of India because of his celibacy; he took a vow of celibacy and got great mental power and determination as a result.
- We don't practice these rules merely to be good moralists (though that is also important); it is scientific. Any instruction Kṛṣṇa gives has a definite purpose. Being detached from illicit connection is advantageous for spiritual life because it gives us the opportunity to think of, and concentrate on, Kṛṣṇa.

### The Devotee's Prime of Life
- For a materialist, the prime of life is youth, when he can enjoy his senses. For a devotee, the prime of life is his Kṛṣṇa conscious maturity, his realizations.
- Therefore, in Vedic culture, after 50 years of age — after so much austerity, penance, and cultivation of devotion — the duty of the senior man was to travel in society and preach Kṛṣṇa consciousness, his realizations now being there. This is a wonderful life. The materialist has nothing to look forward to; he becomes defeated more and more. But the more we practice Kṛṣṇa consciousness, the more glorious it becomes — it is a life of hope, only going to get better, especially if we are strict.
- (At the Sunday Feast he mentioned **existentialism** — realistic, but with little knowledge: "It's just hopeless, so just kill yourself." Kṛṣṇa consciousness means a life of good fortune and hope.)

## Our Strength Lies in Our Strictness

- Sense gratification is defeating: it may be like nectar in the beginning but is poison in the end. In spiritual life there may be a little poison in the beginning (because we have to control our senses) but it is nectar in the end.
- See the world through the philosophical point of view — through the eyes of Śrīla Prabhupāda's purports. Understanding this, a devotee doesn't want to make a compromise; he has been a servant of Māyā for so many lives.
- You surrender to someone when you are defeated by them. But a devotee is not defeated by the material energy, because he is protected by the principles of devotional service.
- **The vows taken at initiation** are great strength: no meat-eating, no intoxication, no illicit sex, no gambling; chanting 16 rounds a day; and taking up active service with a spiritual master. That is sufficient for protection. Even yogīs and jñānīs cannot overcome the material energy — they are ultimately defeated; they rise to the Brahman and fall down. But a devotee becomes the real hero.

### Who Is the Real Hero?
- Not the one who flexes his muscles and throws a steel ball (shot put) halfway across the football field, nor the one who picks up a rubber pigskin and runs into a bunch of men, gets beaten up, and touches it down on the other side of the line.
- The real hero is one who can control his mind and his senses — who can get out of this maximum-security prison. (The movie *The Great Escape*: the Germans captured the American prisoners in a camp with no way out, but the heroes tunneled out and escaped.) The real hero conquers the repetition of birth. We are not impressed by how big your muscles are. Real strength means being able to control the senses — and we all know that takes strength, when you see something attractive (food, or a lady, or a man) and can control yourself. On the basis of that strength one conquers the material energy.

### Don't Compromise
- If we feel weak in devotional service, it may be because we are compromising. If we maintain these principles, there is no reason to feel weak or lack enthusiasm — these principles are our guardians.
- "Give an inch, you'll take a mile of material energy." Therefore devotees are very, very strict. And by being strict we are not the loser — by being strict, we gain.

### Arjuna and Urvaśī — Strictness Without Compromise
- Before the Battle of Kurukṣetra, Arjuna went to see his father, Lord Indra, in the heavenly planets. Indra wanted to entertain him — and entertainment in the heavenly planets is the society girls, the Apsarās. The most beautiful society girl is **Urvaśī.**
- Indra, thinking this would be nice for Arjuna (such things are not considered wrong up there), told Urvaśī to enjoy with Arjuna. Urvaśī approached Arjuna in the dead of night in his chamber, very attractively, and expressed her mind: "I want to unite with you."
- But Arjuna, a man of great devotional principle, was very strict: "You are the property of my father, so you are like my mother. I can only address you as mother. I'm sorry, I can't unite with you."
- She became angry: "No one has ever said this to me before; I am the most beautiful of all society girls." Arjuna: "Mātājī, I'm sorry, but you are my mother."
- A man should see all women as his mother, except his wife. With this mentality one doesn't have this enjoying spirit (unless one is very degraded); mother is always mother. This is why we get into the habit of addressing all the ladies as "Mātājī" (mother) — just as "Prabhu" means master. It establishes a formal relationship. This is good practice; it protects us.

### The Strength of Vows — and Bhīṣmadeva
- A vow means you have to sacrifice. No one comes to this movement a pure devotee. "Money is honey, so sweet it can't be beat" — but if you agree to sacrifice that which is very dear to the conditioned soul (sense gratification), then you become endeared to the person you are sacrificing for. At the moment of initiation you take the vow: no more sense gratification. Then Guru and Kṛṣṇa see "this boy/girl is serious," and mercy comes.
- **Bhīṣmadeva.** His other name is **Devavrata** — "the upholder of great vows," the strength of the demigods.
  - His father was Mahārāja Śāntanu, married to Gaṅgādevī (a heavenly Apsarā), who left him, leaving him without a queen. Śāntanu became attracted to a fisherman's daughter, **Satyavatī**, and wanted to marry her (what is a king without a queen?).
  - His son by Gaṅgādevī — the young Bhīṣma — wanting to serve his father, went to Satyavatī's father the fisherman: "My father would like your daughter as his queen." The fisherman refused: "When she has a son, he will not become king, because your son (being first) will naturally become king. I don't want my grandchildren to take second place."
  - So Bhīṣma said, "All right, for the pleasure of my father, I vow to remain a brahmacārī my entire life." On that condition the fisherman agreed.
  - The moment he spoke that vow, there was thunderous applause from all the demigods; they rained down flowers and gave him the name **Devavrata**, because he took this vow for the pleasure of his father.
- There is great strength to be had from taking these vows on behalf of the spiritual master. Having taken the vow, we remain strong just on the principle of keeping it — the vow keeps us away from sense gratification, sacrificed for the pleasure of Guru and Gaurāṅga; they are pleased, and we get mercy.
- Once someone came to Prabhupāda: "I can't do it anymore, I just can't follow all those rules and regulations." Prabhupāda said only: **"But you promised."** We take that vow at initiation — before Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa, before the spiritual master, before the devotees. It is very serious.
- In many letters Prabhupāda said that even by following just the regular principles and chanting 16 rounds, **"you'll go back to Godhead yet."** Don't underestimate the potency of following these rules; stick to them.

### Arjuna, Draupadī, and the Exile — Māyā's Last Trick
- The five Pāṇḍavas were married to **Pāñcālī** (Draupadī), who had five husbands. They took a vow: when any one of them was with Draupadī, the others would not disturb — and if one did, he would go into exile for some time.
- One day Yudhiṣṭhira Mahārāja was in the tent with Draupadī. During that time a brāhmaṇa came to the Pāṇḍavas' camp in great distress: "Dacoits are stealing my cows! You are the rulers of this land — please protect the cows and the brāhmaṇas; that is your duty as kṣatriyas."
- Arjuna's weapons were in the tent where Yudhiṣṭhira was with Draupadī. Seeing the higher cause of protection, he went in to get his weapons. Yudhiṣṭhira and Draupadī came forward warmly — "Arjuna, so nice you've come" — with no ill feeling. He took his weapons, went out, and smashed the culprits, killing the dacoits.
- But he did not compromise: although Yudhiṣṭhira and Pāñcālī were not upset, he had taken a vow, so he went into exile for a few months just to keep it. He did not think, "It was all right this one time, it's no big deal, they didn't really care anyway."
- **This is Māyā's last trick:** "He's giving me up, so I'll enter his mind in the form of compromise." You've taken the vow, but a little compromise there — and she's still in your heart. So we shouldn't compromise; and we don't have to, because if we don't compromise we'll be spiritually fit, strong, and healthy. **Our strength lies in our strictness.**

## Accept All the Instructions — the Story of Abhimanyu

- It's the same old lecture — no meat-eating, no intoxication — but we have to hear it again, because sometimes we're weak. We have to tighten up.
- We should take all the instructions we get, not accepting some and neglecting others. We advance in Kṛṣṇa consciousness as much as we can absorb every instruction of the spiritual master. Some are prominent, some not, but all are essential.
- **Abhimanyu** (today is "Mahābhārata day"). Abhimanyu was the son of Arjuna and Subhadrā.
  - Sometimes a husband speaks confidentially with his wife ("don't tell anyone" — and the next day it's all over the community: "well, I just told my best friend"). Naturally, Arjuna would sometimes discuss kṣatriya warfare and battle tactics with Subhadrā.
  - The Battle of Kurukṣetra was fought with different **military formations** taking the forms of swans, peacocks, horses, and cows; these formations would fight each other like animals, and one had to change formation according to the formation coming at you.
  - There was one particularly difficult formation (that Arjuna and Bhīṣma knew) — hard to penetrate, with a specific way to get in and a way to get out. Arjuna was explaining this to Subhadrā. Who was listening? **Abhimanyu, in the womb.** He heard how to get *into* the formation — but then Subhadrā fell asleep, so the child never heard how to get *out.*
  - Years later at Kurukṣetra, Bhīṣmadeva organized this formation, and it was necessary to penetrate it. Abhimanyu knew the art of going in, so he went in — but, having been "asleep" for the last part, he couldn't get out of trouble. Though a great warrior (a young boy of 16 whom even the Mahārathīs couldn't defeat individually), he was surrounded by Karṇa, Bhīṣma, Aśvatthāmā, Droṇa, and others, who together defeated him.
- The point: he heard some of the instructions and was successful to a certain degree, but because he didn't understand all of them, he got into trouble.
- So we must accept **all** the instructions of the spiritual master and neglect none. We definitely make progress by accepting the prominent instructions, but every instruction has its purpose — for some aspect of getting freed from the material energy and engaging in devotional service. Prabhupāda's books give instruction for every situation in life; every problem of material existence has a solution, and Kṛṣṇa consciousness is the solution to all problems — "weapons for all the problems of material existence." Assimilate all of them, apply all of them, compromise on none. Neglect an instruction and we may, like Abhimanyu, find ourselves unable to get out of the formation.
- Prabhupāda has given us all the facility to go back home, back to Godhead. If we are serious about following those instructions, we'll make it — and it won't be very difficult.
- **Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī:** "I see no reason that all my disciples could not go back to Godhead in this very lifetime." So be strict, and don't think you can't — everyone can be strict. Being strict means we'll get the strength to do what is necessary. No compromise — just like Bhīṣma, just like Arjuna.
